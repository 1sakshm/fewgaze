FewGaze

How little labelled data does eye-contact detection really need?

FewGaze measures how classifiers that decide whether a person in a photo is looking at the camera improve as the number of labelled examples grows. All four approaches use frozen models: interpretable facial geometry, DINOv2 embeddings, a pretrained gaze estimator (L2CS-Net) and CLIP. Every approach is evaluated on people it has never seen, with identical folds, identical training samples and subject-level confidence intervals.

01 · Question

What is being classified, exactly?

Detecting when someone looks at a camera is useful for video calls, social robots and human–computer interaction. Collecting labelled gaze data is slow, though, and models that look accurate on the people they were trained on often fail on new faces. FewGaze asks a narrow question: for each kind of representation, how many labelled examples per class does it take to recognise camera-directed gaze in people who were never in the training set?

The label is camera-directed gaze under controlled capture conditions. In the Columbia Gaze Data Set (Smith et al., UIST 2013), each subject's head rested in a chin rest. A camera at five positions photographed them looking at a 7 × 3 grid of targets defined relative to that camera. An image is positive when the gaze offset is (0°, 0°), which the authors call gaze locking, and negative for the other 20 offsets, the closest of which is 5° to the side.

This is not interpersonal eye contact. Nobody in these images is talking to anyone. Camera-directed gaze in a lab is a controlled proxy, and every conclusion on this page is limited to it.
02 · Methods

Four representations, one tiny classifier

Every backbone is frozen. Only a regularised logistic regression with fixed hyperparameters is trained on the k labelled examples. All methods read the same MediaPipe face crop.

03 · Protocol

Built so that nothing leaks

Subject-independent folds

The people are dealt into five folds. Each row is one fold; the highlighted dots are its held-out test subjects. Nobody appears on both sides, and every person is tested exactly once.

Label budgets

k examples per class, drawn only from training subjects, nested across budgets and repeated for five seeds. Every method gets the same examples. Bar height uses a log scale; ALL is roughly 225 positives and 4,480 negatives.

Fixed before results

The classifier settings, the 0.5 threshold, the prompts and the paired comparisons were committed to git before the first experiment ran. There was no tuning on test subjects.

Metrics with honest uncertainty

AUROC (primary) and balanced accuracy, averaged over 25 fold × seed runs. The 95 % intervals come from a subject bootstrap with 1,000 replicates, and test labels stay clean in every condition.

06 · Limitations

What this study cannot tell you

  • Camera-directed gaze ≠ eye contactThe subjects looked at a lens or a dot in a lab, not at a conversation partner.
  • 56 people, one labOne background, one distance (2 m), a chin rest, and head yaw only. Generalisation beyond this setting is unknown.
  • Unequal prior knowledgeL2CS-Net was trained with gaze labels on Gaze360, so its k undercounts the supervision it received.
  • No fairness analysisThe dataset has no per-subject demographic labels, so subgroup performance was not and cannot be evaluated here.
  • Label definitionThe negatives 5° to the side sit close to the positives, and individual fixation accuracy is unknown. One duplicated image pair with conflicting labels was found and removed.
  • Not for monitoringGaze detection can be misused for surveillance or attention scoring. These models are research artefacts and must not be used for such purposes.

08 · About

About this project

FewGaze is an independent research project. The work is in the protocol: the folds and training samples are identical across methods, the analysis plan was committed before any result existed, the uncertainty respects subject identity, and the report is limited to what the data supports. It does not claim a new model.

Code MIT. The Columbia Gaze Data Set © Columbia University is used for non-commercial research only (Smith, Yin, Feiner & Nayar, UIST 2013). This page does not show any dataset images.
