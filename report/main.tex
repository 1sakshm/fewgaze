\title{\Large\bfseries How Many Labels Does Eye-Contact Detection Need?\\[2pt]
\large A Subject-Independent Comparison of Geometric Features, Visual Foundation Models,\\ and Gaze Estimators, and Where Gaze Lives Inside Foundation Models}

\begin{abstract}
\noindent
How many labelled images does a model need to recognise that a person it has never seen is looking into the
camera? We study this question on the Columbia Gaze Data Set (5{,}878 images of 56 people after an audit removed a
duplicated pair with conflicting annotations) under a protocol designed to rule out leakage: identity-disjoint folds,
one shared set of training images per budget ($k\in\{5,10,25,50,100,\mathrm{ALL}\}$ per class, five seeds), a
subject-level bootstrap, and an analysis plan committed to version control before the first experiment.
\emph{Study~1} compares four frozen representations with a fixed logistic-regression head. Nine MediaPipe eye and
head measurements with a quadratic boundary are the most label-efficient at every budget, reaching AUROC $0.537$ at
$k{=}5$, $0.730$ at $k{=}100$ and $0.810$ with all data. A DINOv2 linear probe stays at chance ($\le 0.513$). The
L2CS-Net gaze estimator scores $0.595$ without any labels, but a head trained on its two angles never improves on
this, because its outputs carry a head-pose-dependent bias. A 5\textdegree{} gaze shift moves the iris by only
$0.025$ eye-widths, against a between-person spread of $0.017$, which explains why no method separates the gaze-locked
condition from its nearest neighbours.
\emph{Study~2} (pre-registered) probes every layer of five frozen foundation models (DINOv2-S/B, CLIP, SigLIP, MAE)
from the summary token and from eye-region patch tokens. The summary token encodes head pose (final-layer $R^2$ up to $0.97$) but never horizontal gaze ($R^2{\le}0.00$ at every layer of every model). Mean eye-patch tokens in the middle layers do decode gaze (best-layer $R^2(V)$ $0.45$--$0.68$), and much of it is lost by the last layer. When the layer is chosen on training people only, reading eye contact out of CLIP's decoded gaze matches the geometry features (AUROC $0.827$ vs.~$0.816$, paired difference $+0.011$, fold SD $0.019$). This readout uses continuous gaze labels.
\emph{Study~3} asks a generative VLM (Qwen2.5-VL-3B) the question directly. Zero-shot it is the best label-free method (AUROC $0.601$, CLIP $0.547$), but its answers follow head pose more than gaze, and two in-context examples per class lower it to $0.547$.
A browser demo reproduces the Python pipeline to within $4\times10^{-4}$ in probability.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Knowing when someone looks at a camera matters for video-mediated communication, attentive user interfaces and
social robots. Prior work detects eye contact with a camera~\cite{smith2013gazelocking}, with arbitrary objects
without supervision~\cite{zhang2017everyday}, with mobile devices in everyday life~\cite{bace2020quantification}
and between people in group conversations~\cite{muller2018robust,muller2021multimediate}. Each new setting needs
labelled examples, and the value of each label depends on the representation that consumes it. Generic foundation
models such as DINOv2~\cite{oquab2024dinov2} and CLIP~\cite{radford2021clip} are often expected to be
sample-efficient. Dedicated gaze estimators encode gaze explicitly~\cite{abdelrahman2023l2cs,cheng2024survey}.
Hand-crafted eye geometry is cheap and interpretable~\cite{kartynnik2019facemesh,ablavatski2020iris}. How these
compare when labels are scarce, and when evaluation is on \emph{new people}, has rarely been measured directly.

We study a deliberately narrow target: \textbf{camera-directed gaze under controlled capture}, the ``gaze locking''
condition of Smith et al.~\cite{smith2013gazelocking}. It is a proxy for eye contact, not interpersonal eye contact.
Our contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep0pt
\item a leakage-tested, subject-independent label-budget protocol in which every method shares folds, training
images and test images, with the plan committed before the first run and an exact rerun from a clean commit;
\item learning curves, paired comparisons, label-noise, head-pose and per-person analyses for four representation
families (Study~1);
\item a mechanistic account of the failures: the size of the 5\textdegree{} signal relative to person-to-person
variation, identity-dominated foundation-model embeddings, and a head-pose-dependent bias in a pretrained gaze
estimator;
\item a pre-registered, layer- and token-resolved probe of five frozen foundation models for gaze under
held-out-subject evaluation, linked to eye-contact classification (Study~2);
\item a pre-registered test of a generative vision-language model, asked zero-shot and with in-context examples (Study 3);
\item an open, parity-tested in-browser demonstrator.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related work}
\paragraph{Eye contact and gaze locking.}
Smith, Yin, Feiner and Nayar introduced \emph{gaze locking}, passive detection of camera-directed gaze, together
with the Columbia Gaze Data Set; they classified rectified eye patches with PCA, MDA and an SVM~\cite{smith2013gazelocking}.
Zhang, Sugano and Bulling detected eye contact with arbitrary targets by discovering gaze targets without
supervision~\cite{zhang2017everyday}; Sugano, Zhang and Bulling aggregated many viewers' gaze to estimate audience
attention on public displays~\cite{sugano2016aggregaze}. B\^ace et al.\ quantified everyday attention to mobile devices
with eye-contact detection on front-camera video~\cite{bace2020quantification}. M\"uller, Huang, Zhang and Bulling
combined gaze and speaking behaviour to detect eye contact in multi-person conversation~\cite{muller2018robust},
M\"uller et al.\ detected low rapport in small groups from non-verbal behaviour including gaze~\cite{muller2018detecting},
and the MultiMediate challenge established eye-contact detection as a benchmark task~\cite{muller2021multimediate}.
None of these works measures sample efficiency across representation families under a shared protocol.

\paragraph{Appearance-based gaze estimation.}
Sugano et al.\ trained person-independent 3D gaze estimators from synthesised eye images~\cite{sugano2014learning}.
Zhang et al.\ introduced MPIIGaze for gaze estimation in the wild~\cite{zhang2015mpiigaze,zhang2019mpiigaze}, showed
that full-face input with spatial weights improves accuracy~\cite{zhang2017fullface}, revisited the role of data
normalisation~\cite{zhang2018revisiting}, compared appearance-based estimators with commercial eye trackers and
discussed implications for applications~\cite{zhang2019evaluation}, and released ETH-XGaze for extreme head poses~\cite{zhang2020ethxgaze}.
Gaze360 provides unconstrained 360\textdegree{} annotations~\cite{kellnhofer2019gaze360}, on which L2CS-Net trains
per-angle binned classifiers~\cite{abdelrahman2023l2cs}. Park et al.\ estimated gaze from learned eye-region
landmarks~\cite{park2018learning} and, in FAZE, adapted a person-specific estimator from as few as three calibration
samples~\cite{park2019faze}. Cheng et al.\ survey and benchmark the field~\cite{cheng2024survey}. Our geometry
baseline uses MediaPipe face-mesh and iris landmarks~\cite{lugaresi2019mediapipe,kartynnik2019facemesh,ablavatski2020iris}.

\paragraph{Foundation models and gaze.}
Self-supervised and image--text models (DINOv2~\cite{oquab2024dinov2}, CLIP~\cite{radford2021clip},
SigLIP~\cite{zhai2023siglip}, MAE~\cite{he2022mae}) are widely used as frozen backbones. El Banani et al.\ probed such
models for 3D awareness~\cite{elbanani2024probing}. For gaze, UniGaze argues that general-purpose self-supervised
models designed for semantic tasks transfer poorly and pre-trains MAE-style on faces instead~\cite{qin2026unigaze};
CLIP has been used to improve gaze generalisation through language~\cite{yin2024clipgaze}; and a frozen DINOv2 encoder
supports gaze-\emph{target} estimation~\cite{ryan2025gazelle}. Closest to Study~2, a recent preprint probes frozen
DINOv2, CLIP and SigLIP features for gaze on MPIIFaceGaze with a random image split~\cite{shkolnikov2026geometry}.
We add held-out-subject evaluation, every layer, a contrast between the summary token and eye-region patches, and
the link to eye-contact classification.

\section{Data and protocol}
\paragraph{Dataset and label.}
In Columbia Gaze~\cite{smith2013gazelocking}, 56 people sat in a chin rest 2\,m from a camera that moved between five
positions (head poses $0^\circ$, $\pm15^\circ$, $\pm30^\circ$ relative to the camera) and fixated a $7\times3$ grid of
targets defined relative to that camera ($H\in\{0,\pm5,\pm10,\pm15\}^\circ$, $V\in\{0,\pm10\}^\circ$; Fig.~\ref{fig:teaser}).
Positives are $H{=}V{=}0$; the other 20 cells are negatives. Our audit confirmed 5{,}880 readable $5184{\times}3456$
images in a complete grid, and found one byte-identical pair with conflicting horizontal-gaze annotations (subject
0007); both images were excluded before modelling, leaving 280 positives and 5{,}598 negatives (1\,:\,20).
Fig.~\ref{fig:composition} summarises the composition.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{fig2_dataset_composition.pdf}
\caption{Dataset after the audit: images per head pose, labelled images per gaze cell, and per-person class counts.}
\label{fig:composition}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Splits, budgets and selections.}
Subjects are dealt into five folds with a fixed seed (11--12 test people per fold); automated tests verify
disjointness, coverage and determinism. For each fold and seed $s\in\{0..4\}$, one permutation per class of the
training people's images is drawn and its first $k$ images are used. Budgets are therefore nested, and \textbf{every
method receives the same images}. ALL is every training-person image (about 225 positives and 4{,}480 negatives).

\paragraph{Representations (frozen) and classifier.}
\emph{Geometry} uses nine MediaPipe features (Fig.~\ref{fig:teaser}). \emph{DINOv2-S}~\cite{oquab2024dinov2} uses the CLS
token of a 448\,px face crop resized to $224^2$. \emph{L2CS-Net}~\cite{abdelrahman2023l2cs} (ResNet-50, Gaze360
weights) gives (pitch, yaw) from the $448^2$ crop; we also score it without labels as minus the angle to the camera
axis. \emph{CLIP} ViT-B/32~\cite{radford2021clip} is scored zero-shot with two prompts fixed in advance. Every trained
method uses scikit-learn~\cite{pedregosa2011sklearn} logistic regression (C$=$1, balanced weights, threshold 0.5)
inside a pipeline fit on training people only; hyperparameters were never tuned. Because the positive class is the
\emph{centre} of gaze space, no linear function of a monotone gaze proxy can isolate it; a degree-2 expansion was
therefore prespecified for the low-dimensional geometry and L2CS features, and embeddings get a linear probe.

\paragraph{Metrics and uncertainty.}
AUROC (primary) and balanced accuracy are averaged over 25 fold$\times$seed runs. 95\% intervals come from a
fold-stratified \emph{subject} cluster bootstrap (1{,}000 replicates) computed in closed form
($\mathrm{AUROC}=c^\top A c/[(c\cdot P)(c\cdot N)]$ for subject counts $c$; tested against explicit resampling to
$10^{-12}$). The same resamples are used for every method, so differences are paired. Intervals reflect which people
were tested, not retraining variability, which we report separately as the SD across runs.

\paragraph{Learning curves.}
Geometry led at every budget (Fig.~\ref{fig:lc}, Table~\ref{tab:auroc}): AUROC $0.537$ [0.529, 0.545] at $k{=}5$,
$0.601$ [0.592, 0.613] at 25, $0.730$ [0.715, 0.751] at 100 and $0.810$ [0.791, 0.833] with all data (balanced accuracy
$0.527$, $0.578$, $0.686$, $0.733$). Its curve was also the steepest, gaining 0.19 AUROC between 5 and 100 labels.
All three prespecified paired comparisons excluded zero at every budget (Fig.~\ref{fig:forest}); for L2CS$-$DINOv2 at
$k{=}5$ the lower bound is only just above zero. The DINOv2 linear probe stayed at chance ($0.499$--$0.513$). L2CS
angles with a trained head rose from $0.512$ to $0.596$, matching but not improving on its zero-label score
($0.595$ [0.566, 0.630]). CLIP zero-shot reached $0.547$ [0.535, 0.563]. Geometry needed between 10 and 25 labels per
class to overtake the zero-label gaze estimator.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{fig_forest.pdf}
\caption{Prespecified paired differences ($\Delta$AUROC) at every budget, on identical bootstrap resamples.}
\label{fig:forest}

\paragraph{Ablations.}
Removing the polynomial left geometry near chance ($0.504$--$0.555$), confirming the centre-of-gaze-space argument.
L2CS's 2048-d penultimate features with a linear probe ($0.633$ at ALL) beat its two output angles, and neither DINOv2
mean-patch tokens ($\le 0.545$) nor a CLIP probe ($\le 0.567$) approached geometry (Fig.~\ref{fig:ablations}).

\paragraph{The 5\textdegree{} problem.}
At $k{=}25$, geometry called 71.8\% of true positives camera-directed, but also 67.7\% of the $|H|{=}5^\circ$
negatives, 50.8\% of $|H|{\ge}10^\circ$ negatives and 43.2\% of the $V{=}\pm10^\circ$ negatives
(Fig.~\ref{fig:heat}). The reason is visible in the raw measurements (Fig.~\ref{fig:violin}): moving the gaze from
0\textdegree{} to +5\textdegree{} shifts the median iris by $0.025$ eye-widths, while people already differ by $0.017$
(interquartile range) at 0\textdegree. This is consistent with the $\pm2.5^\circ$ horizontal tolerance that Smith et
al.\ estimated for their own detector~\cite{smith2013gazelocking}.

\includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{fig_iris_violin.pdf}

The gaze-locked cell (red) overlaps heavily with its $\pm5^\circ$ neighbours.}

\end{figure*}

\paragraph{Head pose and label noise.}
With all data, geometry's AUROC fell from $0.891$ at a frontal pose to $0.835$/$0.847$ at $\mp15^\circ$ and
$0.718$/$0.766$ at $\mp30^\circ$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:pose}). Flipping training labels degraded geometry roughly in
proportion to the flip rate (at $k{=}100$: $0.730\to0.695/0.656/0.602$ for 10/20/30\%; Fig.~\ref{fig:noise}); at
$k{=}25$, 10\% noise ($0.582$) cost less than reducing the clean budget to $k{=}10$ ($0.564$).

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{fig6_head_pose.pdf}
\caption{AUROC within each head pose (56 positives per pose); hollow markers use no labels.}
\label{fig:pose}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Why the gaze estimator saturates.}
Fig.~\ref{fig:l2cs} plots L2CS-Net's raw outputs. Yaw tracks horizontal gaze, but at the gaze-locked target its
mean ranges from $+1.6^\circ$ (head $-30^\circ$) to $-15^\circ$ (head $+30^\circ$): part of what it reports as gaze is
head pose. Pitch tracks vertical gaze with a constant offset of roughly $-6^\circ$ to $-9^\circ$. Per person, the
zero-label score ranges from AUROC $0.32$ to $0.94$ (SD $0.14$, versus $0.05$ for geometry; Fig.~\ref{fig:subject}),
the signature of person-specific calibration error, which a pooled two-feature classifier cannot remove and
which per-person adaptation in the style of FAZE~\cite{park2019faze} targets. The two methods fail on
different people (Fig.~\ref{fig:subject}), which suggests, but does not establish, complementarity.

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{fig_l2cs_bias.pdf}
\caption{\textbf{L2CS-Net is biased by head pose.} Mean frozen L2CS outputs on Columbia versus true gaze, per head
pose. An unbiased estimator would pass through (0, 0) at the red line for every pose.}
\label{fig:l2cs}
\end{figure*}

\paragraph{Why the foundation model fails.}
A $t$-SNE of the embeddings (Fig.~\ref{fig:tsne}) shows DINOv2 collapsing each person into one tight cluster, split
further by head pose: the nearest neighbour of an image is the same person in $>99.9\%$ of cases (86\% for geometry).
A ridge decoder recovers head pose from the final CLS token with held-out-subject $R^2{=}0.95$ but not gaze ($R^2{<}0$).
Feeding DINOv2 an eye-region crop instead (a post-hoc analysis) raised AUROC at ALL only from $0.513$ to $0.562$.
Study~2 asks whether gaze is present anywhere inside such models.

\section{Study 2: where does gaze live inside foundation models?}
\label{sec:study2}

\paragraph{Design (pre-registered).}
We froze DINOv2-S/14, DINOv2-B/14~\cite{oquab2024dinov2}, CLIP ViT-B/16~\cite{radford2021clip},
SigLIP-B/16~\cite{zhai2023siglip} and MAE ViT-B/16~\cite{he2022mae} (MAE with no masking and identity patch order),
fed each the same $224^2$ face crop with its own normalisation, and cached two representations at every one of 13
layers: the summary token (CLS; SigLIP has none, so the mean patch) and the mean of the patch tokens over each eye
(eye boxes from MediaPipe landmarks re-detected on the crop, padded by 25\% of eye width). A ridge decoder ($\alpha$ by
internal cross-validation) predicted $H$, $V$ and head pose on held-out people, using the same five folds; a linear
logistic probe and a geometric readout $-(\hat H/\sigma_H)^2-(\hat V/\sigma_V)^2$ scored eye contact. The readout
uses continuous gaze labels, which is more supervision than Study~1's binary labels. The nine geometry features were
probed identically as a reference.

\paragraph{The summary token encodes head pose, not gaze.}
Across all five models and all 13 layers, the summary token never decoded horizontal gaze better than a constant:
its best held-out $R^2(H)$ was $0.00$, and from the middle layers onward it was negative (Fig.~\ref{fig:s2heat}, left).
The same tokens decoded head pose well: final-layer $R^2(P)$ was $0.95$ (DINOv2-S), $0.92$ (DINOv2-B) and $0.97$ (MAE).
The two language-aligned models lose head pose in their last layers ($R^2(P)$ $0.25$ for CLIP and $0.56$ for SigLIP,
down from about $0.9$ in mid layers; Fig.~\ref{fig:s2layers}, right). Read out as eye contact, summary tokens stayed
between $0.48$ and $0.64$ AUROC at every layer. The final DINOv2-S CLS token gave a linear-probe AUROC of $0.514$, matching
Study~1's $0.513$ at ALL.

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig_s2_heat.pdf}
\caption{Horizontal-gaze $R^2$ for every model and layer. Left: summary token (never above $0.00$). Right: eye-patch
tokens. Colour is clipped to $[0, 1]$.}
\label{fig:s2heat}
\end{figure*}

\paragraph{Eye-patch tokens carry gaze, mostly in the middle layers.}
Averaging the patch tokens over the two eyes changes the picture (Table~\ref{tab:s2}, Fig.~\ref{fig:s2layers}).
At the patch-embedding layer, eye tokens decode nothing ($R^2(H)$ between $-0.07$ and $-0.02$). By the middle of
the network they decode both gaze angles. The best layer reached $R^2(H)=0.21$--$0.58$ and $R^2(V)=0.45$--$0.68$. Every model's best
layer exceeded the geometry features on vertical gaze ($0.44$), and none reached them on horizontal gaze ($0.78$).
For four of the five models, gaze information peaks at layers 3--8 and then declines towards the output. CLIP's
$R^2(H)$ falls from $0.58$ (layer 6) to $0.33$, SigLIP's from $0.48$ (layer 3) to $0.05$, and DINOv2-B's from $0.46$
(layer 6) to $0.18$. MAE, trained to reconstruct pixels, is the exception: its $R^2(H)$ rises almost monotonically
to $0.47$ at the last layer. This is consistent with the view that the training objective decides whether fine,
task-irrelevant geometry survives to the output~\cite{elbanani2024probing}. Head pose is decodable from the eye tokens
at every layer ($R^2(P)\ge0.91$ after layer 0), so the two quantities are entangled in the same tokens.

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig_s2_readout.pdf}
\caption{Eye-contact AUROC on held-out people by layer, mean over five folds. Left: linear logistic probe on binary
labels. Right: gaze readout from the ridge decoder (trained with continuous gaze labels). Dotted lines show the geometry
features under the same probe: $0.555$ (left) and $0.813$ (right).}
\label{fig:s2readout}
\end{figure*}

\paragraph{Sensitivity to the choice of $\alpha$ (S2-b).}
The pre-registration claimed that choosing $\alpha$ by leave-one-image-out could only inflate $R^2$. This was wrong:
images of the same person remain in the inner training set, which favours weak regularisation that does not transfer
to new people. We recorded the correction and re-ran the eye-token decoder with $\alpha$ chosen by person-grouped inner
cross-validation (last column of Table~\ref{tab:s2}). $R^2$ rose at almost every layer. The best $R^2(H)$ became
$0.51$--$0.64$ (DINOv2-S: $0.21\to0.51$), and final-layer values rose to $0.18$--$0.55$. The geometry reference did not move
($0.776\to0.777$). The qualitative conclusions hold under both procedures: gaze is present in eye tokens and absent
from summary tokens, CLIP's eye tokens decode most, and no model reaches geometry on $H$. The primary analysis
underestimated the absolute level for high-dimensional representations.

\paragraph{From decodable gaze to eye contact.}
A linear logistic probe on the eye tokens, trained only on binary labels, reached at most $0.72$ AUROC (CLIP, layer 9;
Fig.~\ref{fig:s2readout}, left). The geometric readout, which reuses the ridge decoder's $\hat H, \hat V$, did much better
(right panel). Its best layer per model ranged from $0.71$ (DINOv2-S) to $0.83$ (CLIP, layer 8), and the geometry features
scored $0.81$. The best-layer figures are selected on test folds, which our plan excluded. In a post-hoc analysis (P3)
we therefore chose the layer and $\alpha$ inside each training fold by person-grouped CV. Under this procedure, CLIP selected layer 8 in four folds and layer 6 in one, and reached AUROC $0.827$ (fold SD $0.016$). DINOv2-B reached $0.777$, SigLIP $0.769$, DINOv2-S $0.732$ and MAE $0.727$. Geometry, run through the identical procedure, scored $0.816$ ($0.014$). CLIP's paired difference from geometry was $+0.011$ (fold SD $0.019$; positive in four of five folds), which five folds cannot distinguish from zero. The other models trailed geometry by $0.04$--$0.09$, and DINOv2-S, SigLIP and MAE did so in every fold. Two caveats apply. The eye boxes come from the same MediaPipe landmarks that the geometry features use, and the readout is trained on continuous gaze angles. So this is evidence that the information is present, not a label-free alternative to geometry.
Gaze is linearly present in mid-level patch tokens of every model we probed. A generic classifier on the summary token,
the common way of using these models, cannot reach it.

\section{Study 3: asking a generative vision-language model}
\label{sec:study3}
\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig_s3_vlm.pdf}
\caption{Study~3. Left: label-free methods on held-out people (fold-mean AUROC, 95\% subject-bootstrap interval).
Centre: share of ``Yes'' answers by true gaze, zero-shot and with two examples per class in context, on the same
1{,}400 images. Right: zero-shot ``Yes'' rate by head pose, for images looking at the camera and all others.}
\label{fig:s3}
\end{figure*}

\paragraph{Design (pre-registered).}
CLIP scores images against fixed captions; a generative VLM can instead be asked the question in plain language. We
asked Qwen2.5-VL-3B-Instruct, the smallest current open model that fits a 4\,GB laptop GPU (loaded in 4-bit), ``Is
this person looking directly at the camera? Answer with Yes or No.'' The input was the same face crop as every
Study~1 method. The score was the logit of ``Yes'' minus that of ``No'' at the first answer token. Zero-shot (S3-a)
covered all 5{,}878 images under the Study~1 folds and bootstrap. In the few-shot condition (S3-b), two labelled
examples per class from the fold's training people were placed earlier in the conversation. These were the first two
of Study~1's $k{=}5$ sample. S3-b used every positive and four negatives per positive in each test fold
(1{,}400 images). Prompt, example order and subset were fixed before any output was seen.

\paragraph{Zero-shot, the model is the best label-free method, but only just.}
Asked directly, the VLM reached AUROC $0.601$ [$0.568$, $0.633$] (Fig.~\ref{fig:s3}, left). This is better than
CLIP's two prompts ($0.547$; paired difference $+0.054$ [$0.023$, $0.080$]) and level with the L2CS-Net angle score
($0.595$; $+0.006$ [$-0.033$, $0.040$]). It is also above geometry trained on five labels per class ($0.537$).
Taken as a yes/no detector it is poor: it said ``Yes'' to 27\% of all images, where only 4.8\% are positive, for a
balanced accuracy of $0.568$.

\paragraph{What the model answers.}
The ``Yes'' rate follows head pose more than gaze (Fig.~\ref{fig:s3}, right). For people looking at the camera, it
fell from $71\%$ with a frontal head to $16$--$18\%$ at $\pm30^\circ$; for all other images, from $48\%$ to
$11$--$15\%$. On the 1{,}400-image subset the model said ``Yes'' to $40\%$ of camera-directed images and to $38\%$ of
those $5^\circ$ off (centre). Like every other method here, it cannot resolve the hardest distinction. The model
appears to answer partly ``is the face turned towards me?'', which is the same head-pose shortcut that limits
L2CS-Net.

\paragraph{In-context examples hurt.}
Two labelled examples per class lowered AUROC on the same images from $0.603$ to $0.547$ (paired difference
$-0.056$ [$-0.081$, $-0.026$]). This is level with geometry trained on five labels per class ($0.540$ on this subset).
The examples made the model say ``Yes'' less often (19\%), but not more selectively: $21\%$ for camera-directed images
against $23\%$ at $5^\circ$. Small VLMs are known to use in-context images weakly. Four examples of one fine-grained
distinction do not seem to teach it; they mostly shift the model's bias. Larger models, other prompts and more
examples could behave differently. We tested one of each, by design.

\section{An in-browser demonstrator}
We exported the geometry classifier trained on all 5{,}878 images and re-implemented the feature code in JavaScript on
MediaPipe tasks-vision 0.10.21 (the same face-landmark model file as the Python pipeline). On 24 images resized
exactly as in Python, the browser matched Python to $5.5\times10^{-6}$ in landmark coordinates, $2\times10^{-3}$ in
features and $4.3\times10^{-4}$ in probability, with no decision flips; the test also revealed that the library's
transformation matrices are column-major, a detail whose mistreatment flipped 13 of 24 decisions. The demonstrator
runs on camera frames or photos entirely on the client, flags inputs outside the training ranges, and states that it
is a research artefact, not a detector.

\section{Discussion}
Under this protocol the most label-efficient representation was the simplest: nine geometric measurements with the
right inductive bias for a target that sits at the centre of gaze space. Generic self-supervised embeddings were not
label-efficient here because their dominant variation is identity and head pose, both irrelevant to the task. The gaze
estimator shows the value and the limits of prior knowledge: it beats every method trained on ten labels or fewer
without seeing a single FewGaze label, but its person- and pose-dependent bias caps what a pooled classifier can learn
from its outputs. Study~2 locates the failure. Gaze is present in the mid-level eye-patch tokens of all five models, but the summary token drops it, and most models attenuate it in their final layers. The usual recipe, a linear probe on the last CLS token, therefore looks in the wrong place~\cite{elbanani2024probing}. Study~3 adds that asking a small generative VLM is a reasonable zero-label baseline, and that a handful of in-context images does not turn it into a few-shot learner for this distinction. No method resolved the dataset's hardest distinction,
0\textdegree{} versus 5\textdegree, and absolute performance (AUROC $\le0.81$) is far from deployable; it is not
comparable to the per-image accuracy reported by Smith et al., who evaluated a different detector under a different
protocol~\cite{smith2013gazelocking}.

\paragraph{Towards interpersonal eye contact.}
This study is a deliberately controlled first step. The natural next step is social video with real eye-contact labels, such as the group conversations of M\"uller et al.~\cite{muller2018robust,muller2021multimediate}. There, the target is another person, not the camera; heads move freely; and labels come per frame, not per posed photograph. We would carry over three things unchanged: subject-disjoint folds with nested label budgets, a subject-cluster bootstrap, and a pre-registered analysis plan. Studies~1 and~2 make two concrete predictions for that setting. First, eye-local representations (the geometry features, or mid-layer eye-patch tokens) should beat global embeddings. Second, a small amount of per-person calibration should matter more than more pooled labels, because the remaining error is person-specific. Testing both needs temporal models and target-relative gaze, which this dataset cannot provide.

\section{Limitations and ethics}
Results hold for one controlled dataset: 56 people, one lab, 2\,m, yaw-only head pose, a chin rest. Camera-directed
gaze is a proxy, not interpersonal eye contact, and individual fixation accuracy is unknown. Intervals capture
subject sampling with models held fixed. Heads were fixed in advance; other probes might rank the embeddings
differently. Study~3 tested one small VLM (3B parameters, 4-bit), one prompt and one set of examples; larger or full-precision models may do better. The dataset has no demographic labels, so we make no fairness claims, and pretrained models carry the
biases of their training data. The dataset licence forbids redistribution, so no participant image appears in this
paper. Gaze sensing can be misused for surveillance or attention scoring; our models are research artefacts and are
unsuitable for monitoring or high-stakes use.

\section{Conclusion}
A handful of interpretable measurements with a quadratic boundary made the best use of labels, from 5 per class to
several thousand; a frozen generic embedding with a linear probe did not learn the task, and a pretrained gaze
estimator mainly helped when no labels were available. Inside foundation models, gaze is not absent but misplaced. Mid-layer eye-patch tokens carry it, and read out through decoded gaze, CLIP's tokens match nine hand-built measurements. Asked in words, a small generative VLM is the best label-free method, but it answers mostly about the head, and its examples do not help. The open question is whether
person-specific calibration or eye-local representations can resolve the last 5\textdegree{} that separate looking
\emph{at} the camera from looking just past it.

\paragraph{Reproducibility.}
Every number, table and figure is regenerated by the commands in the repository from the recorded runs; the plans for
all three studies were committed before they ran; images are not redistributed.
